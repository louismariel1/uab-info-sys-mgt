Introduction — Centralized Hospital Laundry Location Project

# 1\. Introduction

## 1.1 Problem Definition

The objective of this project is to determine the most suitable location for a **centralized industrial hospital laundry serving hospitals in Vallès Occidental, Catalonia**.

Hospital laundries are responsible for processing large quantities of textiles used in healthcare facilities, including bed linen, towels, patient clothing, healthcare uniforms, and other reusable textile products. The operation requires a reliable system for collecting soiled textiles from hospitals, transporting them to a centralized laundry facility, washing and processing them under appropriate hygienic conditions, and subsequently returning clean textiles to the hospitals.

The location of the laundry facility is therefore an important strategic decision. A facility that is poorly located could generate unnecessarily high transportation costs, longer delivery times, and increased logistical complexity. Conversely, an appropriately located facility can consolidate laundry operations while maintaining efficient access to the hospitals it serves.

The location problem can be formulated as follows:

> **Given a set of hospitals distributed throughout Vallès Occidental, determine the location of a centralized industrial laundry that provides an efficient balance between transportation distance, operating cost, accessibility, facility characteristics, and service coverage.**

The project will not only identify a theoretical optimal geographical point. The final solution must correspond to a **real industrial premise that is currently available and suitable for adaptation to the proposed hospital laundry activity**. Consequently, the project combines quantitative location models with practical real-estate and operational considerations.

---

## 1.2 Objective

The main objective of the project is:

> **To identify and justify the best available location for a centralized industrial hospital laundry serving the hospitals of Vallès Occidental.**

To achieve this objective, four complementary facility-location approaches will be applied:

1. **Center of Gravity Method** – to identify the theoretical geographical location based on the distribution and relative demand of the hospitals.
2. **Factor Rating Method** – to compare candidate premises according to qualitative and quantitative factors such as accessibility, cost, infrastructure, and suitability.
3. **Cost Model** – to estimate and compare the total economic cost associated with operating the laundry from each candidate location.
4. **Graph-Based Models** – including the Simple Median Model and Coverage Model, to evaluate locations according to the road network, weighted transportation distances, and required service coverage.

The results of these methods will subsequently be compared in order to identify the most robust candidate.

A final practical validation will then be performed on the selected premise, considering its:

- physical characteristics and available surface;
- industrial classification and permitted activity;
- accessibility for laundry collection and distribution vehicles;
- availability of utilities and infrastructure;
- rental or purchase cost;
- potential for adaptation and expansion;
- proximity to the hospitals; and
- current availability in the real-estate market.

## 1.4 Why a Centralized Hospital Laundry?

The project assumes that the hospitals are served by **one centralized industrial laundry rather than maintaining separate laundry facilities at each hospital**.

A centralized model can provide several potential operational advantages.

### 1.4.1 Economies of Scale

A single industrial laundry can concentrate washing, drying, ironing, folding, storage, and quality-control operations. This can allow the facility to use industrial-scale equipment more efficiently than several smaller hospital laundries.

### 1.4.2 Specialization

A dedicated industrial laundry can be designed specifically around healthcare textile processing, allowing the operation to establish standardized procedures for:

- separation of soiled and clean textiles;
- washing and disinfection;
- textile handling;
- quality control;
- packaging;
- storage; and
- distribution.

### 1.4.3 Reduced Duplication of Infrastructure

If each hospital operates its own laundry, each location potentially requires its own industrial washing equipment, dryers, finishing equipment, storage space, maintenance, and specialized personnel.

A centralized facility can consolidate these resources.

### 1.4.4 Better Utilization of Capacity

Laundry demand varies between hospitals and over time. A centralized facility can pool demand from several hospitals and potentially achieve higher utilization of its equipment and workforce.

### 1.4.5 Logistics Become a Location-Planning Problem

The principal disadvantage of centralization is that textiles must be transported between the hospitals and the laundry.

This creates two important logistical flows:

$$
\text{Hospitals} \rightarrow \text{Central Laundry}
$$

for soiled textiles, and:

$$
\text{Central Laundry} \rightarrow \text{Hospitals}
$$

for clean textiles.

Consequently, **the location of the centralized facility becomes critical**. The potential economies of scale must be balanced against the additional transportation distances and costs.

This trade-off is the central issue addressed by the location models in this project.

## 1.5 Initial Project Approach

The project will therefore proceed in two main stages.

### Stage 1 — Quantitative Location Analysis

The hospitals will be represented as demand points, weighted initially by their number of beds. Candidate industrial locations will then be evaluated using:

$$
\boxed{\text{Center of Gravity}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Factor Rating}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Cost Model}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Simple Median + Coverage Models}}
$$

### Stage 2 — Real-World Validation

The results will be compared with the actual industrial premises available in the most promising areas.

The final recommendation will be a **specific real premise**, rather than simply a municipality or theoretical coordinate.

This distinction is important because the mathematically optimal location may not be commercially or operationally feasible. The final decision must therefore balance the **modelled optimum** with **real-world feasibility**.
